The CMS rules

Most hospitals must publish both a comprehensive data file and a consumer-friendly way to view common prices. The two requirements serve different purposes.

Rule 45 CFR Part 180
In force Since 2021
Update cycle At least yearly

Who is covered

The rule follows hospital licensing

For this rule, a hospital is an institution licensed as a hospital under state or applicable local law, or approved as meeting those licensing standards.

That definition is broader than Medicare participation. A facility does not enter or leave the rule simply because it accepts Medicare. The relevant question is how the institution is licensed or approved in its jurisdiction.

CMS deems certain government and tribal facilities compliant with Part 180. The regulation names federally owned hospitals, including Department of Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense facilities; hospitals operated by an Indian Health Program; and state forensic hospitals that exclusively treat people in the custody of penal authorities.

Why a registry does not settle every case

This tracker begins with a federal hospital roster because it provides stable identifiers and locations. Licensing, closures, mergers, new campuses, ownership changes, and public websites can change on different schedules, so some records need review.

What hospitals publish

Two duties, two audiences

Part 180 requires a comprehensive machine-readable file and a consumer-friendly presentation of a smaller group of shoppable services.

45 CFR 180.50

Comprehensive machine-readable file

A CSV or JSON file containing all standard charges for all hospital items and services. It includes gross, cash, payer-specific negotiated, and de-identified minimum and maximum charges.

45 CFR 180.60

Consumer-friendly shoppable services

A searchable display, or a qualifying price estimator, for services that can usually be scheduled in advance. The requirement covers as many of 70 CMS-specified services as the hospital offers, plus hospital-selected services for a combined total of at least 300 when available.

The consumer-friendly side must help people search by description, billing code, and payer, unless the hospital uses a qualifying estimator. An estimator can satisfy this part if it covers the required services, provides an estimate of the amount owed to the hospital, is prominent, and works without a charge or account.

The comprehensive MRF is a different product. It is intended for direct download and computer processing, and can contain millions of records. A hospital can publish a strong patient estimator while its MRF has a problem, or publish a working MRF while the patient-facing display has a problem.

Publication and access

The file has to be public in a practical sense

A technically public URL is not enough if a person or automated system must pass through barriers to reach the data.

No payment or account
The MRF must be available free of charge and without creating a user account or entering a password.
No personal information
Access cannot depend on submitting personally identifying information.
Direct and automated access
The file must be available to automated searches and direct downloads through a link on a public website.
Searchable data
The MRF and the standard charge information inside it must be digitally searchable.
Standard filename
The filename follows the CMS convention: <ein>_<hospital-name>_standardcharges.csv or .json.
Website signposts
The hosting site maintains a root /cms-hpt.txt pointer and a footer link labeled “Price Transparency” that leads to the source page.

The hospital also has to identify the location associated with the charges. If locations under one license use different standard charges, each location must separately publish the charges that apply there.

Both the comprehensive file and the consumer-friendly information must be updated at least once each year. The MRF records its most recent update date, and the consumer-facing publication must clearly display its update date.

Timeline

The rule has become more specific over time

The main obligation began in 2021. Later changes standardized how hospitals publish and describe the data.

  1. Jan. 2021
    Publication requirements took effectHospitals began publishing comprehensive standard-charge files and consumer-friendly information for shoppable services.
  2. Jan. 2024
    Websites gained standard signpostsThe hosting website became responsible for a root /cms-hpt.txt pointer and a footer link labeled “Price Transparency.”
  3. July 2024
    CMS templates became requiredMRFs had to conform to a CMS template layout, data specification, and data dictionary.
  4. Jan. 2025
    Additional detail became requiredThe template added remaining data elements for contract methods, allowed amounts, drug units, and code modifiers.
  5. Jan. 2026
    The current requirements took effectThe file moved to an attestation, added a responsible senior official and organizational NPIs, and replaced the estimated allowed amount with 10th percentile, median, and 90th percentile allowed amounts plus a count.
  6. Apr. 2026
    CMS enforcement of the 2026 elements beganThe CMS technical guide identifies April 1, 2026 as the enforcement date for the new template elements.

This timeline is about federal requirements. A file created under an older template may still open normally, but being readable is different from using the current required format.

Enforcement

CMS makes the compliance determination

CMS can evaluate complaints, review outside analyses, conduct audits, request certification or documents, and perform a comprehensive review.

If CMS concludes that a hospital is not complying, the agency may issue a written warning. A material violation can lead to a requested corrective action plan that identifies the changes and timeline needed. If a hospital does not respond to that request or does not complete the approved plan, CMS may impose and publicize a civil monetary penalty.

The regulation says those actions generally, but not necessarily, occur in that order. CMS may also publicize assessments and enforcement outcomes, and it may work with health-system leadership when similar problems affect hospitals across a system.

An outside audit is evidence, not enforcement

This project can record that a public file was missing, blocked, stale, or formatted with an older template at a particular time. It cannot determine legal liability, order a correction, or impose a penalty.

This tracker

The audit checks a defined slice of the rule

The automated check focuses on whether a comprehensive MRF can be discovered and read from outside the hospital's website.

It starts with a known hospital domain, requests the standard pointer location, matches a pointer entry to the hospital, follows the listed MRF URL, and reads enough of the file to inspect basic metadata. The check considers access, declared template version, and the date of the latest update.

It does not validate every record in the MRF. It does not confirm that all negotiated rates are present, evaluate whether the values are accurate, compare the file with contracts, or test the separate shoppable-services display or estimator.

Some hospitals are matched to a file through reviewed evidence even when a usable pointer is unavailable. Those findings retain the source and review context rather than pretending the path was discovered in the standard way.

Reading results

The labels describe what the audit observed

Compliant

A charge file opened and passed the tracker's current access, template, and freshness checks. The prices inside were not verified.

Not compliant

The observed publication had a specific issue such as a missing pointer, dead MRF link, update date older than one year, or outdated template.

Blocked

The website or file refused the automated request. That prevents a result and is kept separate from an observed content failure.

Not assessed

No usable domain was available, the site was unreachable, or a system pointer could not be matched confidently to the hospital.

Exempt

The roster record falls into a category the project treats as outside the ordinary publication check under 45 CFR 180.30(b).

Results are a dated snapshot. Hospitals can replace files, repair links, change domains, or reorganize systems after a crawl. The tracker keeps blocked and unassessed records visible so the coverage limits remain part of the result.
