Hospital price files

One downloadable file holds a hospital's list prices, cash prices, and negotiated insurance rates. CMS calls it a machine-readable file, or MRF.

The basics

One file, many prices

The file is a public data set, not a patient-facing price list. It can contain millions of rows because the same service may have a different rate for every insurance plan.

Each row identifies an item or service, its billing code, whether it is inpatient or outpatient, and one or more prices. The file also identifies the hospital, the template version, and the date of the last update.

Plain answer

The file shows the prices a hospital publishes. It does not show the final amount on a patient's bill.

Prices

The five price types

No single number represents “the price.” CMS requires five kinds of standard charge for each item or service.

List price

The starting amount before discounts. This is often called the gross charge.

Cash price

The amount offered to a patient paying without insurance.

Negotiated price

The amount agreed with a named insurer and plan.

Lowest price

The lowest negotiated rate for that item, without naming the plan.

Highest price

The highest negotiated rate for that item, without naming the plan.

Some contracts use a percentage or formula instead of a fixed dollar amount. The file must identify that method and may also include past payment amounts.

Format

CSV or JSON, using the CMS template

CMS defines the field names, order, and allowed values. Hospitals may use a tall CSV, a wide CSV, or JSON, but the underlying information is the same.

The header names the hospital and locations covered by the file. It also includes the update date, template version, identifiers, and an attestation that the information is accurate and complete.

CSV files may look unusual in spreadsheet software because metadata can appear above the main table. JSON files arrange the same information as nested objects. Both formats are intended for software and bulk analysis.

Limits

This is not your final bill

A negotiated rate is the amount agreed between a hospital and an insurance plan. It is not necessarily the patient's share.

Deductibles, coinsurance, network status, and out-of-pocket limits all affect what a patient owes. Separate bills may also come from physicians, laboratories, or other providers.

For a personal estimate, use the hospital's estimator or contact its billing office. The tracker only checks whether the public file can be found, opened, and appears current; individual prices are not verified.
