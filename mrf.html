Hospital price files

Hospitals publish a downloadable record of list prices, cash prices, and negotiated insurance rates. CMS calls it a machine-readable file, or MRF.

The basics

A public data set, not a price menu

A machine-readable file is the hospital's most complete public price data. It is designed so software can download and analyze the information in bulk.

The file covers the items and services for which the hospital has established a standard charge. That can include supplies, procedures, room and board, facility fees, service packages, and some professional services provided by clinicians employed by the hospital. A hospital may need separate files for locations that operate under one license but use different standard charges.

These files are often very large. One service can be repeated for many insurance companies, plans, care settings, billing codes, and contract methods. A file with millions of records is not unusual, even though the hospital may offer far fewer distinct services.

The MRF is one of two required publications

The comprehensive MRF is built for bulk access. A separate rule covers a consumer-friendly display or estimator for shoppable services. Meeting one requirement does not automatically satisfy the other.

Reading the data

What a record describes

A useful price record needs enough context to identify the service, the setting, the payer and plan when relevant, and the way the charge was established.

Item or service
A description such as an imaging study, laboratory test, supply, drug, room charge, or bundled service.
Billing code
A hospital code or a standard code such as CPT, HCPCS, DRG, NDC, or a revenue code. A service can carry more than one code.
Care setting
Whether the charge applies to an inpatient admission, an outpatient department visit, or both.
Payer and plan
The insurance company and plan tied to a negotiated charge. A plan category may be used when the same rate applies across that category.
Charge method
Whether the rate is a dollar amount, percentage, algorithm, or another contract method.

A billing code helps align similar services across files, but it does not guarantee that two records describe the same clinical situation. Location, setting, modifiers, units, and the contents of a service package can all change what is being priced.

Standard charges

Five different answers to “what is the price?”

The regulation defines five types of standard charge. They answer different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable.

Charge typeWhat it representsWhat to keep in mind
Gross chargeThe chargemaster amount before discounts.Often called the list price. It may be much higher than the amount usually paid.
Discounted cash priceThe charge offered to someone paying cash or a cash equivalent.It is not necessarily the only self-pay option or a personalized estimate.
Payer-specific negotiated chargeThe charge agreed with a named third-party payer and plan.There may be many negotiated entries for the same service.
De-identified minimumThe lowest negotiated charge across third-party payers for the item or service.The payer and plan behind the minimum are not identified.
De-identified maximumThe highest negotiated charge across third-party payers for the item or service.The payer and plan behind the maximum are not identified.
 The five standard charge categories are defined in 45 CFR 180.20.

The minimum and maximum give a range, not a typical price. They also do not reveal how many rates fall near either end. The payer-specific records carry more detail, but comparisons still require compatible codes, settings, units, and contract methods.

Contract methods

Not every negotiated rate starts as a dollar amount

Some contracts set a fixed dollar rate. Others use a percentage, fee schedule, formula, or algorithm that depends on information available when a claim is processed.

The file must identify the method used. When a negotiated charge cannot be expressed as a dollar amount or known in advance, the 2026 requirements call for the information needed to derive the amount when that information is available to the hospital.

For those non-dollar arrangements, the current template also carries historical allowed-amount statistics: the 10th percentile, median, 90th percentile, and the number of remittances used in the calculation. These describe what the hospital historically received for a payer and service over the required lookback period. They are not a guarantee of a future payment.

A blank field needs context

A blank dollar amount can reflect missing data, a rate that does not apply, or a contract that uses another method. The companion fields are necessary before treating the blank as an error.

File structure

CMS supplies the layout and data dictionary

Since July 2024, hospitals have had to use a CMS template. The allowed forms are tall CSV, wide CSV, and JSON.

Tall CSV

More rows, fewer columns

Charge types and payer rates are represented vertically. This is often easier for databases but can produce a very long file.

Wide CSV

More information across columns

Related values are spread across a wider row layout. Spreadsheet software may still struggle with the file's width and size.

JSON

Nested structured data

Hospital, service, code, and rate information is grouped into objects and arrays rather than table cells.

Each format includes file-level information such as the hospital name, locations and addresses, license information, CMS template version, and the date of the latest update. Current 2026 requirements also add organizational NPIs, an attestation, and the name of the senior official responsible for overseeing the file's accuracy and completeness.

CSV metadata may appear above the main data table, so opening the file in a spreadsheet can look unfamiliar. JSON represents the same concepts through nested records. The CMS data dictionary, rather than the visual appearance in a spreadsheet or text editor, is the reliable guide to interpreting either format.

Limits

A published rate is not a final bill

A negotiated charge describes an agreement between a hospital and a health plan. It does not, by itself, state what a particular patient will owe.

Deductibles, coinsurance, copayments, network status, benefit limits, and the services actually delivered all affect patient responsibility. A hospital file may include professional charges for employed clinicians, but separate bills can still come from independent physicians, laboratories, anesthesiology groups, ambulance services, or other providers.

The files are still valuable. They make negotiated rates available for research, market comparisons, contract analysis, and public oversight. For an individual estimate, the hospital's patient-facing tool and the health plan's benefit information are usually more relevant.

There is also a difference between a file being technically reachable and the data being complete or correct. Opening a current-format file does not prove that every required rate is present, every code is accurate, or every contract has been represented properly.

This tracker

The audit checks publication, not every price

The tracker follows public links and reads a bounded portion of the file to answer a narrow set of questions.

It checks whether the file can be found and opened, whether the declared template version is current, and whether the reported update date is within the annual window. It does not download and validate every rate in every file. It also does not test the separate consumer-facing estimator or shoppable-services display.

That is why a tracker status should be read as an observation about public access and basic file metadata. It is useful evidence, but it is not a CMS compliance determination and it is not a judgment about the prices themselves.
